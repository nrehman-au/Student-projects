Category 3 Machine learning for time-series data

Learning from sensor, health, energy and industrial signals

A time series is an ordered sequence of measurements. A wearable device may record acceleration every fraction of a second, an ECG contains voltage measurements from several leads, and an industrial plant records pressure, flow, temperature and energy over time. Machine-learning models can use these signals for classification, forecasting, anomaly detection and condition monitoring.

Many projects in this category use self-supervised learning (SSL) because useful labels may require expert work and are often scarce. SSL creates a training task from the unlabelled recordings: a model might align two valid views of a signal, reconstruct a hidden segment or predict a future representation. Other projects train directly for forecasting and study how a model represents time, channels and relationships among variables.

Choose a task that interests you: recognising activities, understanding health or industrial signals, or forecasting future measurements. Each project offers a focused comparison rather than requiring several new models at once.

Learn first and find ideas

  • TS2Vec paper and code for learning from unlabelled recordings.
  • SimMTM paper and code for masked time-series learning.
  • Mamba code and installation requirements for sequence modelling.

3.1 Recognising activities from wearable sensor data

LevelBSc only ComputingStart with a small encoder; a GPU can help with training.

Goal

Recognise activities such as walking, sitting and climbing stairs from smartphone motion measurements. Investigate whether learning from unlabelled recordings reduces the amount of labelled data needed to train an activity recogniser.

Data and starting material

Use UCI Human Activity Recognition, which contains motion recordings from 30 people performing six activities. Keep the supplied separation of people between training and test data.

  • TS-TCC paper and authors’ code.

Methods and evaluation

Explore self-supervised time-series learning, especially contrastive learning, which encourages different valid views of the same recording to have similar representations. Compare it with a small neural network trained directly from activity labels.

Evaluate recognition on people not used for training. Compare performance with small and larger labelled training sets to determine how much labelled data pretraining can replace.

Bachelor focus. How many activity labels can be saved through self-supervised pretraining? Include a comparison without pretraining and explain which activities are hardest to recognise.

3.2 Learning from ECG recordings with fewer labels or leads

LevelMSc preferred; BSc with a supplied data pipeline ComputingA GPU is recommended; begin with the lower-resolution recordings.

Goal

Learn useful patterns from 12-lead ECG recordings and use them to classify recorded diagnostic categories. Investigate whether learning to recover hidden leads helps when few labels or fewer leads are available.

Data and starting material

Use PTB-XL, which provides 12-lead ECG recordings and diagnostic annotations. Use its official patient-aware training, validation and test folds. Smaller label budgets will simulate limited annotation availability.

  • SimMTM paper and authors’ code.
  • PTB-XL preprocessing and supervised benchmark.

Methods and evaluation

Explore supervised time-series models and masked self-supervised learning, in which the model learns from hidden parts of a recording. For the Master route, compare hiding complete leads with hiding short time segments.

Evaluate on patients kept separate from training. Compare diagnostic classification with different amounts of labelled data or available leads, and examine performance across diagnostic categories.

Bachelor focus. Using the supplied pipeline, study how classification changes when fewer ECG leads are available. Compare supervised training with one established pretraining method.

Master extension. Develop lead-aware masking and test whether it improves learning from limited labels compared with ordinary temporal masking.

3.3 Transferring learned representations between energy time series

LevelBSc or MSc ComputingA GPU is useful; start with an hourly dataset and a small model.

Goal

Learn patterns from one electricity-transformer time series and reuse them to forecast another. Investigate whether self-supervised pretraining improves predictions when only a small amount of data is available from the new transformer.

Data and starting material

Use the hourly ETTh1 and ETTh2 subsets of the Electricity Transformer Temperature dataset. They record oil temperature and load measurements from different transformers.

  • CoST paper and authors’ code.
  • DLinear forecasting reference.

Methods and evaluation

Explore self-supervised time-series learning and simple forecasting models. Start with representations that capture trend and seasonal patterns, and compare them with a forecaster trained directly on the target series.

Train on earlier periods and evaluate predictions on later periods. Compare forecast error with and without transfer, using the same forecast horizons and target-data availability.

Bachelor focus. Does pretraining on one transformer improve forecasting for another, especially when target training data are limited?

Master extension. Develop a way to account for changing seasonal patterns during pretraining. Test whether it improves transfer when patterns shift over time.

3.4 Learning heat-pump behaviour from smart-meter data

LevelMSc preferred; reduced BSc version possible ComputingA compact model is suitable after preparing the household recordings.

Goal

Learn patterns in household electricity use and investigate whether they help identify a heat-pump attribute or an issue recorded during an inspection. Test whether unlabelled households can improve predictions for a smaller group with labels.

Data and starting material

Use HEAPO on Zenodo, which provides electricity recordings from 1,408 Swiss households, weather information and inspection data for a smaller subset. Select one target after checking how many households have usable labels.

  • HEAPO paper and dataloader.
  • TS2Vec representation-learning code.

Methods and evaluation

Explore self-supervised time-series learning and conventional classifiers using consumption summaries, weather or metadata. Compare learning from unlabelled recordings with training directly on the available labels.

Keep each household entirely within one data partition. Compare classification on unseen households with different amounts of labelled data, and report uncertainty when the labelled sample is small.

Bachelor focus. Use a well-covered metadata label, such as heat-pump type, to compare supervised and self-supervised learning.

Master extension. Explore learning that accounts for weather or operating cycles. Test whether it improves prediction for the smaller labelled household group.

Practical note. The linked HEAPO dataset is released under CC BY 4.0. Credit the dataset authors and confirm that the selected target has enough labelled households before allocation.

3.5 Learning from graphs built from bearing vibration signals

LevelMSc only ComputingSignal preparation is the main initial workload; use a compact graph model.

Goal

Turn bearing vibration recordings into graphs whose nodes describe useful frequency regions. Investigate whether relationships informed by bearing behaviour, together with learning from unlabelled recordings, help recognise faults under different speeds or loads.

Data and starting material

Use the Paderborn Bearing Data Center, which provides measurements from 32 bearings under four operating conditions. Each signal window becomes one graph.

  • GraphMAE paper and authors’ code.

Methods and evaluation

Explore signal-processing features, graph neural networks and masked graph learning, which learns by recovering hidden graph features. Compare a graph based on bearing knowledge with one based on statistical similarity.

Keep measurements from each physical bearing separate between training and test sets. Compare fault recognition across operating conditions, and test the separate value of graph connections and self-supervised pretraining.

Master extension. Develop a graph representation that accounts for changing bearing speed. Test whether it transfers between operating conditions better than similarity-based graphs, and whether pretraining provides an additional benefit.

3.6 Learning bearing faults with time–frequency models and Mamba

LevelMSc preferred; BSc with prepared data and a fixed comparison ComputingA GPU is recommended; check the Mamba software requirements early.

Goal

Learn from both vibration waveforms and their frequency content to recognise bearing faults. Investigate whether a Mamba sequence model improves transfer between operating conditions when only a small labelled dataset is available.

Data and starting material

Use the Paderborn bearing dataset. The TF-C repository links to prepared fault-diagnosis data that can support initial experiments; verify their bearing or recording identities before using them for transfer claims.

  • TF-C paper, code and prepared-data instructions.
  • Mamba sequence-model code.

Methods and evaluation

Explore time–frequency contrastive learning and sequence models such as convolutional networks and Mamba. Begin with the established TF-C approach and change one encoder component.

Compare fault recognition across operating conditions and with different amounts of labelled data. Keep related recordings separate, and measure both recognition quality and computational cost.

Bachelor focus. Using prepared data, does replacing the existing encoder with Mamba improve recognition or efficiency under the same learning setup?

Master extension. Develop a signal view that accounts for bearing frequencies or changing speed. Test whether it improves transfer to different conditions and, where identities are available, unseen bearings.

3.7 Using relationships between signals to improve Mamba forecasts

LevelMSc preferred; reduced BSc evaluation version possible ComputingStart with Weather; Electricity requires a larger experiment.

Goal

Forecast several related measurements together, such as weather variables or electricity demand. Investigate whether explicitly connecting related signals improves a Mamba forecaster and makes its predictions less sensitive to the order in which variables are presented.

Data and starting material

Start with the Weather benchmark available through Autoformer. The Electricity benchmark is a possible follow-up once the first experiment works.

  • MambaTS paper and authors’ code.
  • DLinear forecasting reference.

Methods and evaluation

Explore simple forecasting methods, Mamba sequence models and graph-based sharing of information between variables. For the Master route, build connections using relationships estimated from training data.

Evaluate forecast error on later unseen data. Compare predictions when the variable order changes or some measurements are missing, using consistent changes to inputs and outputs.

Bachelor focus. How sensitive are existing Mamba forecasters to variable order, and does modelling relationships between variables help?

Master extension. Develop a graph module connecting related signals. Test whether it improves forecasting and robustness compared with models that process channels independently or mix them sequentially.
