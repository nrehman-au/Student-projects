Category 1 GNN applications for real-world data

Practical graph prediction problems with public benchmarks

A graph is a way to represent entities and the relationships between them. The entities are called nodes and their relationships are called edges. Both may carry useful information: a customer node might contain age or location, a product node might contain category and price, and an edge might record that a customer bought or rated a product. Road sensors connected by roads, transactions linked by payments and atoms connected by chemical bonds are other examples.

A graph neural network (GNN) learns numerical representations, or embeddings, by combining a node’s own features with information from its neighbours. In the example, the model can use a customer’s previous purchases, the properties of products and patterns from nearby customers to score a new customer-product link. The same principle supports node classification (classify a transaction), link prediction (recommend a product), and graph-level prediction (predict a property of an entire molecule or sensor system).

The projects below begin with a practical decision: estimate remaining life, forecast traffic, detect illicit activity, recommend an item or screen a molecule. Students must define what the nodes and edges mean, choose the appropriate prediction level and test whether the graph adds value beyond a non-graph model.

Application project requirements

Specify a defensible graph construction, a fixed split and primary metric, a non-graph baseline and at least one GNN. Include an edge-removal, edge-randomisation or alternative-graph experiment to test whether the graph contributes.

Learn first and find ideas

  • Begin with A Gentle Introduction to Graph Neural Networks, then use the Stanford CS224W course and project guidance and the CS224W project archive.
  • PyTorch Geometric dataset catalogue, Open Graph Benchmark, SNAP datasets and the Temporal Graph Benchmark.
  • Stanford examples: traffic as a dynamic graph, water quality with RGCNs, fake-news detection and NFT transaction prediction.

1.1 Remaining useful life prediction for aircraft engines

TaskGraph-level temporal regression Recommended levelBSc or MSc ComputeVery light to light; CPU or 4 GB GPU Starting paperHuang, He and Sick, Spatio-Temporal Attention GNN for RUL, 2024

What you will do. Predict how many operating cycles remain before a turbofan engine fails. Each sensor channel becomes a node; edges express statistical or learned relationships among sensors. A temporal encoder then follows how the sensor graph changes as an engine ages.

Project goal. Given a recent window of readings from one engine, predict one remaining-life value. The scientific question is whether explicitly modelling sensor interactions improves over treating the measurements as an ordinary multivariate sequence.

Data. Use NASA C-MAPSS. Start with FD001: 100 training engines, 100 test engines, three operating-condition variables and 21 sensor channels. FD002–FD004 are optional transfer sets. Split by engine, never by windows from the same engine.

Methods and starting code. Linear or MLP regression, GRU/LSTM/TCN, GCN–GRU, GAT–GRU and the anchor spatiotemporal attention model. Compare correlation, random and learned sensor graphs. PHMGNNBenchmark and the GNN RUL benchmarking repository are useful code references; students should still document their own preprocessing.

Required baselines. A naive constant or degradation-stage baseline, one graph-free sequence model and one compact spatiotemporal GNN. The graph experiment must include a no-edge or random-edge control.

Evaluation. MAE or RMSE is primary; the asymmetric NASA score is optional. Report error by early, middle and late life. Fit normalization and graph construction on training engines only and report at least three seeds for MSc work.

Bachelor scope. Use FD001. Compare a GRU with one compact GNN, one graph construction and one random/no-edge ablation. Submit a documented pipeline and state whether the graph changes performance.

Master research question and contribution. Research question: Can a sparse, time-varying sensor graph improve RUL accuracy and robustness when sensors become noisy or unavailable? Develop one graph-learning mechanism, compare it with fixed correlation and random graphs, and include missing-sensor, uncertainty or cross-subset transfer analysis. Merely rerunning an available implementation is not the contribution.

Practical notes. FD001 is easy to run. The main risks are window leakage, inconsistent RUL capping and comparing results produced with different normalization or scoring rules. Freeze these choices before modelling.

1.2 Multi-horizon traffic forecasting with data-driven graph structure

TaskNode-level temporal regression Recommended levelBSc or MSc ComputeLight to moderate; 4–8 GB GPU Starting paperLi et al., DCRNN, and Wu et al., Graph WaveNet

What you will do. Forecast traffic speed at every road sensor 15, 30 and 60 minutes ahead. The supplied road graph is a useful starting point, but congestion can couple sensors in ways that do not follow the shortest road distance.

Project goal. Determine whether a road-distance graph, a training-only correlation graph or a learned graph gives the most reliable forecasts, especially at the 60-minute horizon.

Data. Use METR-LA from the official DCRNN repository: 207 sensors and 34,272 five-minute observations. PEMS-BAY with 325 sensors is the optional transfer target. Use the established chronological 70/10/20 split.

Methods and starting code. Historical average, linear regression, GRU, DCRNN, STGCN, Graph WaveNet code, MTGNN and a compact Transformer. A dynamic graph should be sparse or low-rank so its cost is explicit.

Required baselines. Historical average, graph-free GRU/Transformer and one fixed-graph spatiotemporal GNN. Compare supplied distance adjacency, training-only correlation adjacency, a single learned static adjacency and the proposed variant.

Evaluation. MAE is primary; report RMSE and MAPE at 15/30/60 minutes. Fit scalers and correlation graphs on the training period only. For dynamic adjacency, report memory, latency and edge stability as well as forecast error.

Bachelor scope. Compare historical average, GRU and one spatiotemporal GNN on the supplied graph. Add one no-edge or randomized-edge control.

Master research question and contribution. Research question: Does a time-of-day and traffic-regime-conditioned sparse adjacency improve long-horizon forecasting over both the road graph and a single learned static graph? Match depth and width, use at least three seeds, and examine whether rush-hour edges differ from free-flow edges and whether learned edges correspond to plausible topology. A robustness-and-transfer route using missing sensors and METR-LA to PEMS-BAY transfer is an alternative, but it must be narrowed to one preregistered question.

Practical notes. Per-timestep dense adjacency is unnecessarily expensive and unstable. Profile early and enforce top-k sparsity or low rank. This project and Project 3.1 both use traffic data, but this one studies the graph itself; Project 3.1 studies unseen-sensor representations.

1.3 Illicit Bitcoin transaction detection

TaskNode classification on a temporal graph Recommended levelBSc or MSc ComputeModerate; sparse mini-batches on 4–8 GB GPU Starting paperWeber et al., Anti-Money Laundering in Bitcoin

What you will do. Each Bitcoin transaction is a node, and directed payment flows are edges. The model predicts whether a labelled transaction is licit or illicit while respecting the order in which transactions occurred.

Project goal. Test whether neighbourhood information helps detect rare illicit transactions beyond the transaction’s own features, without allowing information from future time steps to leak into training.

Data. Use the PyG Elliptic Bitcoin Temporal loader: 203,769 transaction nodes, 234,355 edges, 165 features and 49 time steps. Unknown labels are unlabelled, not negatives.

Methods and starting code. Logistic regression or gradient boosting on node features; GCN, GraphSAGE or GAT; EvolveGCN or TGN for an MSc extension. Use class weighting or focal loss only after establishing an unweighted baseline. PyG’s loader is the recommended data starting point.

Required baselines. Feature-only classifier, static GCN/GraphSAGE and a no-edge or random-edge control. A temporal model is required only for the MSc temporal route.

Evaluation. Chronological splits only. Use AUPRC or illicit-class F1 as the primary metric and report illicit recall at a fixed false-positive rate. Exclude unknown nodes from supervised loss and labelled-node metrics; examine performance over time.

Bachelor scope. Compare a feature-only model with GCN or GraphSAGE and analyse how edge removal changes performance. Include class-imbalance and per-time-step error analysis.

Master research question and contribution. Research question: Can a temporally calibrated GNN remain useful under concept drift and missing transaction edges? Add one temporal mechanism or uncertainty method, test edge deletion and temporal shift, and connect threshold choices to investigator workload. A negative result may show that features dominate topology during particular regimes.

Practical notes. Random splitting makes this task unrealistically easy. Keep time ordering, labels and threshold selection fixed across methods. Mini-batching or snapshot processing keeps the graph manageable.

1.4 Graph-based movie recommendation with accuracy and coverage

TaskLink prediction and ranking on a bipartite graph Recommended levelBSc recommended; limited MSc scope ComputeVery light; CPU or low-memory GPU Starting paperHe et al., LightGCN, SIGIR 2020

What you will do. Represent users and movies as two node types. A rating or interaction is an edge. The model scores unseen user–movie pairs and recommends a ranked list, while also checking whether recommendations collapse onto popular titles.

Project goal. Measure whether message passing improves top-k ranking over popularity and matrix factorization and whether any gain comes at the cost of catalogue coverage.

Data. Use MovieLens 100K: 100,000 timestamped ratings from about 1,000 users on 1,700 movies, roughly a 5 MB download.

Methods and starting code. Popularity, item/user nearest neighbours, matrix factorization and LightGCN. The official LightGCN repository is a reference, but its data protocol must be adapted transparently to MovieLens 100K.

Required baselines. Popularity and matrix factorization are mandatory; LightGCN is the graph model. Use identical candidate sets and negative-sampling rules.

Evaluation. Use a per-user chronological split and remove validation/test interactions from the training graph. NDCG@20 or Recall@20 is primary; also report catalogue coverage and average popularity of recommended items.

Bachelor scope. Compare popularity, matrix factorization and LightGCN. Study depth or negative sampling and report both accuracy and coverage.

Master research question and contribution. For an MSc project, use a narrower research question such as: Can a calibrated diversity objective improve long-tail exposure without materially reducing NDCG? This requires user-activity and item-popularity subgroup analysis, multiple seeds and a defined Pareto trade-off. Without this extension, the project is suitable for Bachelor scope.

Practical notes. The dataset and baseline setup are small. The principal pitfalls are leakage from future interactions and evaluation on sampled negatives that differ across methods.

1.5 Molecular HIV activity prediction with calibrated GNNs

TaskGraph classification Recommended levelBSc or MSc ComputeLight to moderate; 4–8 GB GPU Starting paperHu et al., Open Graph Benchmark

What you will do. A molecule is a graph: atoms are nodes and bonds are edges. Predict whether the complete molecule is active in the HIV benchmark assay and test whether confidence estimates remain useful on molecular scaffolds not seen during training.

Project goal. Compare learned graph representations with established chemical fingerprints under the official out-of-scaffold split.

Data. Use OGB ogbg-molhiv: 41,127 molecular graphs, official scaffold split and official ROC-AUC evaluator. The OGB loader guide handles download and split indices.

Methods and starting code. Morgan fingerprints with logistic regression or random forest; GCN; GIN/GINE; PNA; compact GraphGPS. OGB provides baseline code, and PyG provides the layers and loader.

Required baselines. Fingerprint classifier, GCN and GIN/GINE. Bond-feature and pooling ablations are required. If self-supervised pretraining is added, follow the shared SSL protocol.

Evaluation. Use only the official scaffold split and evaluator. ROC-AUC is primary; AUPRC, Brier score or expected calibration error are supporting metrics. Report at least three seeds. Do not compare scaffold-split results with random-split figures.

Bachelor scope. Compare fingerprints, GCN and GIN/GINE and inspect errors on unfamiliar scaffolds. Include one bond-feature or pooling ablation.

Master research question and contribution. Research question: Can uncertainty-aware training improve calibration on held-out scaffolds without sacrificing discrimination? Compare temperature scaling, deep ensembles or a compact evidential/uncertainty variant on identical predictions and report both performance and calibration by scaffold novelty. A self-supervised route is possible, but it must be distinct from Project 3.3.

Practical notes. The dataset is well established and manageable. The main danger is silently changing the split or selecting a threshold on test data.
